# deck-types — what each kind of presentation must cover

Identify the type from what the user asked for, then follow its outline. These sequences
are conventional because audiences expect them — an investor who can't find traction where
traction belongs assumes it isn't there. Adapt to the actual content; don't pad to hit a
slide count, and cut any slide you have no real material for.

**Universal rules, all types:**

- **Slide 1 states the point, not just the title.** The audience should know what this is
  about and why it matters from the first slide.
- **Headlines assert.** "Churn is concentrated in month 2" beats "Churn Analysis". Read
  only the headlines top to bottom — that sequence alone should tell the story.
- **The ask is explicit.** Any deck that wants a decision, budget, or action names it in
  plain words on its own slide. Vague asks get vague answers.
- **Appendix is free.** Detail that would bloat the main line goes after the closing slide.
  A tight 12-slide deck with a 10-slide appendix beats a 22-slide main line.
- **Last slide is Sources** (this app's rule) — note paths, recording names+ids, URLs only
  if the web was actually used.

## 1. Executive / board update
**Audience:** senior leaders, low patience, high context. **Length:** 8–12 + appendix.

1. Headline verdict — the one thing to know, stated as a conclusion
2. Status against the plan — on/off track, in one visual
3. Key metrics — 3–5 numbers max, each vs. target and vs. last period
4. What changed since last update
5. Wins — brief, evidenced
6. Risks & issues — with owner and mitigation, not just a list of worries
7. Decisions needed from this room — the actual point of the meeting
8. What's next — next period's commitments
9. Appendix — full metrics, detail

**Answer-first.** Lead with the conclusion; detail supports it. **Failure mode:** a
chronological activity report that buries the verdict on slide 9, and risks listed with no
owner or mitigation.

## 2. Project kickoff
**Audience:** the delivery team + stakeholders. **Length:** 10–14.

1. Why this project exists — the problem, and the cost of not solving it
2. Objectives & success criteria — measurable
3. Scope: in / out — the out-of-scope list prevents most later conflict
4. Deliverables
5. Team, roles, RACI — who decides vs. who does
6. Timeline & milestones
7. Dependencies & assumptions
8. Risks & mitigations
9. Ways of working — cadence, tools, comms
10. Immediate next steps — named owners and dates

**Failure mode:** no explicit out-of-scope, and no named decision-maker.

## 3. Investor pitch (seed / Series A)
**Audience:** investors. **Length:** 10–14. Every slide earns the next.

1. Company purpose — one clear sentence
2. Problem — whose, how painful, how they cope today
3. Solution — what you do, ideally demonstrated
4. Why now — the shift that makes this possible/urgent now
5. Market size — TAM/SAM/SOM, built bottom-up
6. Product — how it actually works
7. Traction — the make-or-break slide: revenue, growth, retention, real usage
8. Business model — how money is made, unit economics
9. Competition — honest positioning, your durable advantage
10. Go-to-market — how you acquire, and what it costs
11. Team — why this team wins
12. Financials — projections with stated assumptions
13. The ask — amount, use of funds, milestones it buys

**Failure mode:** top-down market sizing ("1% of a $50B market"), a competition slide
where you win every row, and a vague ask. Never invent traction numbers — placeholder them.

## 4. Sales / client pitch
**Audience:** a prospect. **Length:** 8–12. Their problem, not your product tour.

1. Their situation — prove you understand their world
2. The problem & its cost — quantified in their terms
3. The stakes of inaction
4. Your approach — the solution as it applies to *them*
5. Proof — case study, named results, testimonial
6. How it works — implementation, timeline, effort required of them
7. Differentiation — why you over the alternatives (including doing nothing)
8. Pricing & packages
9. Next step — one concrete action with a date

**Failure mode:** opening with your company history and a feature list. Open on them.

## 5. Product launch / GTM
**Audience:** internal go-to-market teams or press/customers. **Length:** 10–14.

1. What we're launching — one line
2. Who it's for — the target segment
3. The problem it solves
4. Key capabilities — 3–5, benefit-framed not feature-framed
5. Demo / what it looks like
6. Positioning & messaging — the words everyone should use
7. Pricing & packaging
8. Launch timeline & phases
9. Channel plan — who does what
10. Success metrics
11. FAQ / objection handling
12. Who to contact

**Failure mode:** feature-listing with no positioning, so every team describes it
differently.

## 6. Quarterly business review (QBR)
**Audience:** leadership, or a customer's stakeholders. **Length:** 12–18 + appendix.

1. Executive summary — the quarter in one verdict
2. Goals set last quarter vs. actual — scored honestly
3. Performance metrics with trend
4. What drove the results — analysis, not description
5. Wins & case studies
6. Misses & lessons — credibility comes from naming these
7. Market/context changes
8. Next quarter's priorities & targets
9. Resource needs / asks
10. Appendix — detail

**Failure mode:** only reporting wins. A QBR with no misses is not believed.

## 7. Technical design review / architecture
**Audience:** engineers, architects. **Length:** 12–18.

1. Problem statement & goals
2. Non-goals — as important as goals
3. Requirements — functional, and the numbers for scale/latency/availability
4. Current state — and why it's insufficient
5. Proposed architecture — the main diagram
6. Key components & data flow
7. Alternatives considered, and why rejected — reviewers judge you on this
8. Trade-offs accepted
9. Failure modes & resilience
10. Security & privacy
11. Migration/rollout plan, including rollback
12. Cost & operational impact
13. Open questions & decisions needed

**Failure mode:** presenting one design as inevitable with no alternatives, and no
rollback story.

## 8. Research findings / analysis
**Audience:** decision-makers. **Length:** 10–16 + appendix.

1. The question asked
2. Headline findings — up front, answer-first
3. Method & data — enough for credibility
4. Finding 1..N — one per slide, each: claim, evidence, so-what
5. Limitations & confidence — what the data can't tell you
6. Implications
7. Recommendations — prioritised, actionable
8. Appendix — full data, methodology

**Failure mode:** methodology first, findings on slide 12. Lead with what you found.
Never present a finding without its evidence.

## 9. Training / educational
**Audience:** learners. **Length:** 15–30, chunked.

1. What you'll be able to do after this — learning objectives as capabilities
2. Why it matters — motivation
3. Prerequisites
4. Agenda / roadmap
5. Concept modules — each: concept → worked example → practice → check
6. Common mistakes & how to avoid them
7. Summary of key takeaways
8. Practice exercises
9. Where to learn more

**Failure mode:** continuous exposition with no chunking, no examples, no checks. Break
every 3–4 slides with something concrete.

## 10. Strategy / vision
**Audience:** the org or its leadership. **Length:** 12–18.

1. Where we are — honest current state
2. What's changing — market forces, the shift
3. Where we're going — the vision, vividly stated
4. Why this, why now
5. Strategic pillars — 3–4, each with an owner
6. What we will NOT do — the credibility slide
7. What has to be true — key assumptions
8. Roadmap / horizons
9. How we'll measure progress
10. What we need from you

**Failure mode:** aspiration with no trade-off. A strategy that sacrifices nothing isn't
a strategy.

## 11. Post-mortem / incident review
**Audience:** the team and stakeholders. **Length:** 8–12. Blameless.

1. What happened — plain summary
2. Impact — users, duration, revenue, quantified
3. Timeline — detection → escalation → mitigation → resolution
4. Root cause — the systemic cause, not the person
5. What went well — detection, response, comms
6. What went wrong — contributing factors
7. Where we got lucky — the near-misses
8. Action items — owner + date + priority for each
9. How we prevent recurrence structurally

**Failure mode:** naming individuals, stopping at proximate cause, and action items with
no owner or date.

## 12. Conference / keynote talk
**Audience:** a broad room, no obligation to care. **Length:** 15–30, sparse slides.

1. A hook — story, surprising fact, provocative question
2. Why this matters to *them*
3. The core idea — one, stated clearly
4. Argument beats — evidence, story, demonstration
5. The turn — the counterintuitive insight
6. What it means for the audience
7. Call to action
8. Contact / resources

**Failure mode:** dense slides read aloud. Conference slides carry a few words or one
image; the speaker carries the content. This is the one type where sparse is right —
though "sparse" still means a *designed* full-bleed visual, never an empty slide.

---

## Blended or unknown type

If the request doesn't match cleanly, pick the closest type and adapt, or compose the
universal spine:

1. Context — where we are, why we're here
2. The problem or opportunity — with its cost
3. The insight or approach
4. The evidence
5. What it means
6. The ask / next step, with owners and dates

State the assumption you made about the type and audience in your reply, so the user can
correct it in one message.
